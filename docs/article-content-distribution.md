## 1. Quora Answer
**Questions to Answer**: 
* *"How do recruitment agencies get clients?"*
* *"What's the best way to get clients for a staffing agency?"*

```text
Most recruitment agencies get clients one of two ways: referrals, which you can't control or scale, and outbound, which you can. The agencies that grow past the referral ceiling all build outbound into a system instead of doing it in bursts when billing slows.

Here's what actually works, from running this for agencies:

Pick a narrow specialty. "We staff everything" is invisible. "We place logistics staff in the Midwest" is findable and referable.

Build a real target list. Named companies hiring in your specialty, with every decision maker identified, not just one contact per company. The one person you pick is often busy or not the buyer.

Reach out about them, not you. "We're a leading agency" gets deleted. A message about a role they just posted gets read.

Go deep on follow-up. Most replies come between the fifth and twelfth touch. Agencies that stop after two emails never reach the point where meetings actually get booked. Twelve to sixteen touches over 45-60 days across email and LinkedIn is the working shape.

Keep it running when you're busy. The pipeline you need in two months is built now. This is the part that breaks, and it's why most agency new-business is feast or famine.

On tools: you can build the stack yourself (roughly $1,500/mo in software plus 100+ hours a month of work), hire an SDR ($4-6k/mo, months to ramp), or run a system one person operates. I build one of those systems (SDR GROW, for recruitment and staffing agencies specifically), so take that as disclosure, but the five points above are what getting clients requires regardless of how you run it.
```

## 2. LinkedIn Article (First-Person Founder Essay)
**Title**: What I Learned About Recruitment Agency Sales From Talking to 50 Owners

```text
Over the last year I talked to a lot of recruitment agency owners about the same problem: they were great at filling roles and stuck at winning new clients.

The pattern was almost always the same. Business development happened in bursts. A slow month would hit, the founder would blast a couple hundred emails, land a call or two, get busy delivering, and stop. Three months later the slow month came back. Revenue looked like a heartbeat monitor.

I started to see recruitment agency sales as an infrastructure problem, not an effort problem. The owners working hardest at outreach often had the worst results, because they were doing it manually, inconsistently, and only when they were already behind.

The agencies that had escaped the cycle had five things in common:

A defined list of target companies, not a vague market. Sending infrastructure that kept them out of spam. A sequence deep enough to reach the fifth-to-twelfth touch where most replies actually come. Outreach tied to something happening in the prospect's world, not their own credentials. And a rhythm that ran whether or not the founder had time that week.

None of that is glamorous. All of it is buildable. That's the whole reason I built SDR GROW, to run those five things as a system for recruitment agencies so one person could do the work of a sales desk.

If you run an agency and your new-business is feast or famine, the fix is almost never "try harder." It's "stop depending on remembering to try."
```

## 3. Medium Article (Analytical & Data-Led)
**Title**: The Real Cost of Recruitment Agency Sales (And Why Most Agencies Get the Math Wrong)  
*(Note: Set canonical URL to https://sdrgrow.com/blog/best-outbound-systems-recruitment-agencies)*

```text
Every recruitment agency has to solve the same equation: how to win new client companies without it costing more than the placements are worth. Most get the math wrong, and here's where.

The three ways agencies do sales, with the real numbers:

Do it yourself with a tool stack. A data source, enrichment, a sender with warming, a LinkedIn tool, monitoring. Roughly $1,500 a month in software. The number nobody counts is labour: list building, sequence writing, deliverability, monitoring, over 100 hours a month. At even $25/hour that's $2,500+ in time. Real cost: around $4,000 a month, mostly hidden in your own hours.

Hire an SDR. $4,000 to $6,000 a month before tools. Three to six months to ramp. And you're paying full cost to find out whether your outbound works at all, with no benchmark. If they quit at month seven, the process leaves with them.

Run a system one person operates. $1,200 to $3,500 a month depending on the provider. Running in days, not quarters. The process belongs to you.

The mistake is comparing the $1,200-3,500 system to the $39 email tool and concluding the system is expensive. That compares a whole operation to one part of one channel. The honest comparison is the system against the fully-loaded cost of doing the job, which is where the tool stack ($4,000 in software plus time) and the SDR hire ($4-6k plus ramp) actually land.

Priced properly, for most agencies under $2M revenue, the system is the cheapest working option, not the expensive one. The trap is counting the software bill and ignoring the hours.

(Disclosure: I build one of these systems, SDR GROW, for recruitment agencies. The math above holds whichever provider you use, or if you build it yourself.)
```

## 5. Reddit Post / Response
**Target Threads**: Agency growth, recruitment client acquisition, B2B sales automation

```text
The thing that changed our client flow was treating outbound as a system instead of something we did when work got slow.

Specifics that mattered: we stopped emailing one contact per company and started reaching every decision layer (the person who ignored us wasn't always the buyer). We extended follow-up way past where we used to quit, most replies came around touch 5-8, and we'd been stopping at 2. And we tied every first line to something the company was actually doing, a new role, an expansion, instead of leading with how great we were.

Reply rate went from around 1-2% to something we could actually build on. Not magic, just depth and consistency where we'd had bursts.
```
